# How Real Productions Build Visual Identity

This reference document distills craft knowledge from the world's most
accomplished cinematographers, production designers, colorists, and costume
designers. Use it to ground your visual identity recommendations in real
production practice.

---

## Camera Selection Shapes the Image at Its Foundation

The camera determines sensor size, dynamic range, color science, resolution,
and the physical shooting experience — all of which ripple through every
subsequent creative decision.

**ARRI Alexa** dominates high-end narrative cinema for its organic color
science and film-like rendering. Roger Deakins drove ARRI to build the Alexa
Mini LF prototype for *1917* because he wanted "the feeling of slightly
shallow depth-of-field that you get from a larger format sensor." Greig Fraser
shot *Dune* on Alexa LF and combined Alexa 65 with Mini LF for *Dune: Part
Two*: "What I love about 65mm is that it removes restrictions for me, opening
up so many more lens options."

**IMAX film cameras** capture 15-perf 65mm negative (~18K resolution
equivalent) but are physically enormous and loud. Hoyte van Hoytema shot
*Oppenheimer* almost entirely on IMAX — "a movie about faces" where "close-ups
become our landscape."

Camera choice reflects philosophy. When Fraser tested everything for *Dune*,
"Film did not impress Denis as much as we thought — Denis felt it had a
nostalgic quality which wasn't what he envisioned Dune to be." Linus Sandgren
chose Super 16mm for *First Man*'s intimate scenes because 35mm "looked too
good. It looked too much like a movie."

## Lenses as the Primary Tool for Authoring a Unique Look

In the digital age, **the lens is the single most important tool for giving an
image personality**. Bradford Young: "Digital film stock is basically all
captured the same, but the lenses are how you give the image its textural
quality."

**Anamorphic vs. spherical** is foundational. Anamorphic lenses produce oval
bokeh, horizontal streak flares, edge distortion, and shallower depth of field
— associated with epic, cinematic storytelling. Robert Richardson selected
different anamorphic lens series shot-by-shot on *Once Upon a Time in
Hollywood*. Spherical lenses produce more naturalistic images with circular
bokeh. Deakins almost exclusively uses sharp spherical primes (Zeiss Master
Primes, ARRI Signature Primes).

**Vintage and "imperfect" lenses** have accelerated as sensors get sharper.
Fraser developed custom ALFA anamorphics for *The Batman* by deliberately
"detuning" Master Anamorphics — the resulting lenses had "a great deal of
astigmatism, uneven field of focus, and some coma at the sides."

**Dan Sasaki at Panavision** is the most sought-after lens modifier: custom
40mm anamorphic for *La La Land* (19-inch close focus), adapted Hasselblad
lenses for *Oppenheimer*'s IMAX cameras. Young takes a "quilting" approach,
mixing multiple lens sets within a film: on *Arrival*, "90% of the movie was
shot with Ultra Prime 28mm and Super Speed 35mm" because "for me it's about
being imperfect."

## Film Stock vs. Digital — Philosophy, Not Just Technology

Kodak is the world's sole manufacturer of color motion picture negative. Their
**Vision3 500T (5219)** appears on 376+ major titles. On *Phantom Thread*,
PTA rated it between 640-800 ASA with a push: "a stock that people keep coming
back to because, with a push, it picks up extra texture."

For *La La Land*, Sandgren overexposed, rating 500T at EI 200 and
pull-processing: "The film stock itself helps create the magic in a real world.
It exaggerates the colors."

The most extraordinary innovation: **IMAX black-and-white negative for
*Oppenheimer*** — a stock that had never existed, engineered by Kodak, FotoKem,
IMAX, and Panavision.

Rodrigo Prieto used **four different film stocks plus digital** on *Killers of
the Flower Moon*: 250D for daylight, 50D for rituals, 500T for tungsten
interiors, Double-X for simulated newsreel, and Sony Venice at 2500 ISO for
oil-lamp interiors.

## Color Grading Builds the Emotional Architecture

The colorist works as a crucial creative partner — part artist, part
psychologist, part technical engineer.

**Process**: Tom Poole creates a LUT for every show, watches footage alone,
makes notes, creates a "colour bible" where the first session is loose —
"setting a couple of shots on each scene." Then strings selected shots into a
sizzle reel: "You start chipping off big blocks, getting the shape, and bit by
bit you slowly start focusing more on detail."

**Custom LUTs**: For *Joker*, Jill Bogdanowicz built a LUT emulating the
discontinued Kodak EXR 200T 5293 stock — "encouraging a somewhat subdued
palette including the subtle cyan tint in the shadows."

**Iconic color palettes**:
- *Breaking Bad*: Systematic color-character mapping — Walter White neutral
  beige→green→black; Jesse yellow; Skyler blue; Hank orange; Marie purple
- *Blade Runner 2049*: Las Vegas orange achieved entirely in-camera with
  custom Tiffen amber filters combining Lee 790 Moroccan Pink and 105 Orange
- *O Brother, Where Art Thou?*: First feature entirely digitally
  color-corrected — Deakins turned green Mississippi foliage into Dust Bowl
  burnt gold
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel*: Bogdanowicz pushed back on desaturation for
  1930s scenes: "If you transported yourself into the 1930s, it wouldn't be
  faded! Let's celebrate the color."

## Lighting Design — Where Craft Becomes Visual Philosophy

**Gordon Willis** ("Prince of Darkness") established the paradigm with *The
Godfather*. His famous top-lighting of Brando transformed a makeup necessity
into a narrative system where faces grew darker as characters descended
morally. He rejected fill light and insisted on **"visual relativity"**: "You
can't spend two hours on a dark palette. So you've got this high-key,
Kodachrome wedding. Now you go back inside and it's dark again."

**Vittorio Storaro** mapped the color spectrum to life stages on *Apocalypse
Now*: "Black is the unconscious; red is blood; orange is warm family; yellow is
consciousness; green is knowledge; blue is intelligence; indigo is material
power; violet is your last stage; white means balance."

**Roger Deakins** takes an aggressively naturalistic approach. For *Blade
Runner 2049*'s Wallace office caustic water-light: **256 ARRI 300-watt
Fresnels in two concentric circles**, programmed to chase in sequence while
10K Fresnels bounced off actual water — all practical, not digital.

**Bradford Young** developed underexposure as artistic and political act: "When
you underexpose dark brown skin tones, they pop and shine in a way you're not
going to see when a face is lit conventionally." On *Ain't Them Bodies Saints*:
day exteriors underexposed three stops with ND9, interiors underexposed one
stop.

**Emmanuel Lubezki** used exclusively natural light for *The Revenant*
(except one campfire scene): "The expanses of land are so big it would have
been ridiculous to try to light them." The production had ~90 minutes of
usable light per day.

## Production Design Creates the Physical World

**Hannah Beachler** (first Black woman to win Production Design Oscar, *Black
Panther*): eight months of research, drew from Zaha Hadid's architecture.
Created a **400-page guide** for *Wakanda Forever*'s Talokan.

**Patrice Vermette** (*Dune*): "method designing — take it out of the fantasy
and anchor it to reality." Used Brutalist architecture to "underline the show
of force of the colonizer, the same way the USSR used it during the cold war."

**Adam Stockhausen** (*The Grand Budapest Hotel*): Anderson starts every film
with "a big colour idea — a bold gesture from the very first sketch carrying
through to the end." When Stockhausen proposed brown dirt, Anderson insisted:
"No, the desert is red, almost Mars red."

**Jack Fisk** learned to **never use white on sets**: "It looks like it burns
a hole in the film. People look much better on dark backgrounds."

## Costume Design and Makeup

**Sandy Powell** insists on the distinction between costume and fashion:
"Costume design is about collaboration and communication — with directors,
actors, your team, and the audience. It is part psychology, part art." On
*Far from Heaven*: "Todd Haynes had a Pantone strip of colour attached to each
scene in the script."

**Ruth Carter** embedded cultural identity into *Black Panther*'s superhero
design: "For the T'Challa suit, the texture needed to connect him to Africa,
so we came up with the triangle pattern — the sacred geometry of Africa."

**Michelle Matland** (*Succession*) codified "quiet luxury" — "nuanced
variations in black, navy blue, and beige tailored to reflect each character's
particular strain of inner turmoil." Research: "standing outside News Corp and
Viacom trying to pick out who the high-powered execs were."

**Kazu Hiro** transformed Oldman into Churchill for *Darkest Hour* through
strategic restraint — prosthetics for cheeks, jowls, neck, nose, chin while
leaving eyes, forehead, and mouth exposed for expression.

## Aspect Ratios, Framing, and Spatial Identity

**Variable ratios as emotional tool**: Wes Anderson used three ratios in *The
Grand Budapest Hotel* — 1.37:1 (1930s), 2.40:1 (1960s), 1.85:1 (present).
Xavier Dolan used 1:1 for *Mommy*, expanding to 1.85:1 during moments of hope.
Robert Eggers chose nearly-square 1.19:1 for *The Lighthouse*: "It's about
being stuck with somebody you can't get away from."

**Framing philosophy** distinguishes directors: Anderson's symmetry is measured
"from the lens to the corners of the room." Fincher's camera is "a soulless
machine" — never handheld, always mechanically precise. Chloé Zhao's
*Nomadland* used wide-angle lenses close to non-professional actors because
"the lens is very sensitive to things that are not authentic."

## The Look Book and Visual Bible

The look book typically contains: reference images from paintings, photographs,
and other films; color palette and lighting references; storyboards; camera and
lens test results; LUT references; costume and production design mood boards.

Reed Morano created a **72-page pitch deck in 72 hours** that won her *The
Handmaid's Tale*. Bradford Young compiles look-books from fine artists like
Romare Bearden and photojournalist Charles "Teenie" Harris.

For television, the visual bible must survive handoffs. On *Game of Thrones*,
"All DPs get iPads at the beginning of the season with frame grabs organized by
set and location for the last several seasons."

## Case Studies in Visual Identity Systems

### Del Toro's Notebooks
"Visual is narrative. Colors, light, design, texture — all of those are
narrative elements." For *Pan's Labyrinth*: strict dual-color system — cold
blues for reality, warm golds for fantasy. Every fantasy sequence mirrors a set
in the real world. The Pale Man emerged from "a mix of accidents, financial
limitations, and great ideas from many different artists."

### Villeneuve's Scale and Restraint
For *Blade Runner 2049*, the Las Vegas sequence was inspired by the 2009
Australian dust storm. Deakins: "You can't do it in post. The way those lights
combine — you can't replicate that." For *Dune: Part Two*'s Giedi Prime,
Fraser pioneered **infrared cinematography** — removing IR filters so "colours
are rendered as different tones."

### Fincher's Digital Precision
Pioneered digital cinema with *Zodiac* (2007). For *Mank*, used RED Ranger
Helium Monochrome 8K (native B&W sensor). Created "black bloom" in DI and
painted in lens flares digitally, allowing "art directed flares."

### Nolan's Analog Immersion
*Oppenheimer*: first IMAX black-and-white photography ever. Color = "Fission"
(Oppenheimer's subjective story). B&W = "Fusion" (Strauss's objective story).
Final IMAX print: 600 pounds, 11 miles long.

### Television Visual Identity
- *Breaking Bad*: Tobacco filter on lens for desert tonality. "I don't pay
  much attention to reality when I light."
- *Euphoria* S2: ~50% shot on Kodak Ektachrome (100 ISO reversal stock) for
  radical texture shift from S1's digital.
- *Chernobyl*: Radiation portrayed through light — "rays of light often
  overexposing parts of witnesses' faces and bodies." Un-rehoused Cooke
  Panchros chosen for "subtlety — they didn't give too strong a look but had
  some soul."
- *Succession*: Shot on 35mm film all four seasons with Leitz Summilux-C
  lenses. "The show is 'non-cinema.' The camera and the audience are a fly on
  the wall."
- *True Detective* S1: "We basically prepped it like one giant movie."
  Six-minute tracking shot took months of planning.

## Core Principles Across All Productions

1. **Pre-visualization is non-negotiable** — del Toro's notebooks, Anderson's
   animatics, Morano's 72-page pitch deck, Deakins' year-long prep for *Blade
   Runner 2049*. No great visual identity is improvised.

2. **The lens is the primary authoring tool** of the digital age — Young's
   "quilting," Fraser's deliberate degradation, the explosion of vintage glass
   and custom modifications.

3. **Color operates as narrative language** at every level — Storaro's
   spectrum-as-life-journey, Gilligan's character-coded wardrobe,
   Bogdanowicz's LUTs emulating discontinued stocks.

4. **Cross-departmental coordination is the mechanism** that transforms
   individual choices into unified identity — the Wakandan Bible shared across
   departments, Powell's Pantone strips, Fisk avoiding white walls.

5. **Obsessive planning + openness to productive accidents** — the Pale Man
   emerged from "accidents, financial limitations, and great ideas." Sandgren's
   film jam in *La La Land*'s epilogue was a camera malfunction. Deakins found
   *Sicario*'s dramatic skies because monsoon season arrived unexpectedly. The
   visual bible provides structure; judgment determines when breaking from it
   serves the story.
