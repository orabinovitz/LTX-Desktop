# What the Masters See When They Close Their Eyes

The greatest cinematographers alive don't think about cameras. They think about what a slant of light does to a human face, why an empty sky can break your heart, and how the distance between two people in a frame tells a story no dialogue ever could. Across hundreds of interviews, forum posts, masterclasses, and memoirs, the world's most decorated directors of photography have articulated a shared secret: the image must be felt before it is seen. "People confuse 'pretty' with good cinematography," Roger Deakins has said. What follows is the collected visual wisdom of more than a dozen master image-makers -- not how they rig a light, but why they choose to let half a face disappear into shadow, where they place a body inside a rectangle, and what color means when it is used like music.

These insights emerge from the philosophies of Deakins, Lubezki, Storaro, Miyagawa, Almendros, Willis, Hall, Zsigmond, Alcott, Richardson, Khondji, Kaminski, Prieto, Pfister, and Alberti -- a century of accumulated visual intelligence, distilled into the principles that govern what's inside the frame.

---

## "Where you put someone in the frame is everything"

Composition is where every cinematographer begins and where the best ones never stop arguing with themselves. Deakins, perhaps the most articulate on the subject, frames it as a matter of emotional psychology: "The balance of the frame -- the way an actor is relating to the space in the frame -- is the most important factor in helping the audience feel what the character is thinking." Not what the character *says*. What they *think*. This is the foundational distinction. The frame is a mind-reading device.

And yet the process of building that frame resists formulas. Deakins is openly skeptical of rigid compositional systems: "There is such a range of possibilities when it comes to composition, choice of lens and camera movement. What is certain is that there is no one way to do things, not even on the same film." He once joked that his compositional instinct might come down to "the breakfast I ate. Chemistry." This isn't false modesty -- it's a recognition that great framing emerges from internalized knowledge, not from overlaying a rule-of-thirds grid on a monitor.

**Symmetry** operates on a deeper register than most filmmakers realize. Kubrick weaponized it: his one-point perspective shots in *The Shining* and *2001* register as subtly wrong because perfect geometric balance in a real environment is unnatural. As one analysis noted, "The symmetry signals control, and too much control implies something sinister beneath the surface. It's a bit like a smile that's too wide for too long." Wes Anderson works the same compositional math toward the opposite emotional result -- his symmetry constructs a storybook world of deliberate artifice.

The **frame within a frame** -- characters seen through doorways, windows, mirrors, archways -- appears across nearly every master's work because it accomplishes something almost impossible: it makes the audience conscious of watching while simultaneously deepening their engagement. Deakins "often frames his characters within structures like windows, hallways, and doorways, not just for beauty but for meaning. These internal frames mirror emotional and psychological barriers." On *Barbie*, Rodrigo Prieto and Greta Gerwig took a radically different approach: the camera was always "frontal or sideways or behind, never oblique angles, with wide-angle lenses so that you feel you're right there, and there's nothing hidden." The innocence of the toy-box world demanded a frame with no secrets.

## Writing stories in color, one wavelength at a time

No cinematographer has thought more systematically about color than Vittorio Storaro, who spent an entire year after *Apocalypse Now* studying the symbology, physiology, and dramaturgy of color -- and emerged with a philosophy that treats the visible spectrum as a musical scale. "If you're aware of it," he explained, "you can use them like a musician using do, re, mi, fa, so, la, ti, do. You're using red, orange, yellow, blue, violet. That's the point."

For *The Last Emperor*, Storaro mapped the color spectrum onto the stages of human life itself: "Red is the first colour in the spectrum and it is the colour of birth, the colour of life. Orange -- when we are between three and ten years old -- is the moment when we need the warm feeling of family around us. Yellow is the colour of consciousness. Green is the colour of knowledge. Blue is the colour of freedom."

Rodrigo Prieto has developed a different but equally sophisticated color philosophy. On *Killers of the Flower Moon*, he deployed the Lumiere brothers' **autochrome process** -- a European color technique -- exclusively for the white characters, while photographing the Osage "in the most naturalistic way possible in terms of color." The choice was philosophical: a colonial technique literally imposed on colonial subjects. On *The Irishman*, he emulated specific film stocks for each era: "a Kodachrome look for the '50s, an Ektachrome look for the '60s, and a bleach bypass look for the '70s onward."

Darius Khondji approaches color as a painter would, literally sitting and staring at Delacroix postcards during prep. His signature palette -- **green and gold** -- recurs across his work. On *Se7en*, he combined "warmer, tungsten Chinese lanterns" with "cooler Kino Flo fluorescent tubes." David Fincher's direction: "Darius, it has to be *scary*." Khondji's response: "It literally sounded like a serial killer was talking to me."

Janusz Kaminski used color absence as a narrative weapon. On *Minority Report*, the bleach bypass process made the film "void of certain colors, just as it's void of certain emotions." On *Bridge of Spies*, the logic was geographic: "The East Germans are more obscure and sketchy, and the good guys, the West German world, is much brighter and cleaner." Wally Pfister color-coded the dream levels of *Inception*: "Calgary has a sterile, cool look; the hallways have warm hues; and the van scenes are neutral. You immediately know where you are."

## Painting pictures in the dark

Gordon Willis earned his nickname -- "The Prince of Darkness" -- by understanding something most cinematographers resist: the power of what you *don't* show. His philosophy: "A cinematographer is a visual psychiatrist, moving an audience, making them think the way you want them to think, painting pictures in the dark." On *The Godfather*, he described the intended look as "a newspaper photograph in bad color." Paramount executives panicked. Willis told Coppola: "Just hang in there. It's going to be OK."

Willis's minimalism was radical: "I'm a minimalist. I like to take things out, not add things in. People don't understand the elegance of simplicity."

**Light as revelation, shadow as concealment** -- this binary is the spine of visual storytelling. Storaro: "All great films are a resolution of a conflict between darkness and light. Light reveals truth, shadows obscure it." Deakins described each frame as beginning "completely black," with the DP introducing light the way "blues guitarists handle the beats between notes."

Kaminski pushes against naturalism: "I like hard light. I like the light to be visible." His signature **backlight** is narrative: "I love backlight not just for the sake of glamorizing the subject, but because the direction of the light can represent storytelling." And he insists on ugly light: "You don't always have to provide beautiful lighting. I often think ugly lighting and ugly composition tell the story much better than the perfect light."

Khondji lives at the extremes: "I can't get enough of this impression of blinding light and absolute blackness." His **oblique top-corner lighting** creates dramatic pockets where "an actor will exit the shadows and hit a small pool of light" at the emotionally critical moment.

Nestor Almendros stood at the opposite pole, insisting on light that is justified and functional: "I believe that what is functional is beautiful, that functional light is beautiful light." His approach on *Days of Heaven* with Malick -- shooting almost exclusively at the **magic hour**: "The light was very beautiful, but we had little time to film scenes of long duration. For those few minutes, the light is truly magical, because no one knows where it is coming from."

## Physical distance is emotional distance

The choice between a close-up and a wide shot is a philosophical decision. As the principle holds: **in cinema, physical distance equals emotional distance.** A wide shot tells us the environment is more powerful than the individual. A close-up tells us nothing exists except this face.

Deakins: "Someone said to me, if you can photograph the human face you can photograph anything, because that is the most difficult and most interesting thing to photograph." Khondji: "The story is told by faces." He considers CinemaScope "the most beautiful format to frame. One can become absorbed in the faces when they're framed in 'Scope."

Prieto uses **shallow depth of field** as subjective experience. In *Babel*, shooting deaf character Chieko, "he chose an anamorphic lens and a wide open aperture to create a shallow depth of field, visually emphasizing Chieko's deafness, blurring out the surroundings she wouldn't be aurally aware of." His methodology is **"subjective cinematography"**: "I try to make the audience be in the perspective of the character."

Maryse Alberti uses shot size to establish hierarchy between character and world. In *The Wrestler*, the **2.4:1 anamorphic ratio** was chosen to "capture the wrestling ring, fans, and the arena" -- then subverted when Randy is dwarfed by grocery store aisles, the wide frame weaponized against him.

## When to move the camera -- and when to let the world be still

Deakins cites Bresson: "Watch a Bresson! He doesn't move the camera because he can't find a reason to do it, so why should we?" When Deakins does move -- the crane in *Shawshank*, the silhouette march in *Sicario* -- the movement carries the weight of all the stillness that preceded it.

Pfister describes the mechanics: "There is no more simple, more emotional move in film language than to push slowly into somebody's face." The slow push-in works because it mimics the psychological act of closing emotional distance.

Lubezki operates from the opposite impulse -- the camera as a living body. His metaphor for working with Malick: "We are fishermen trying to get little bits from a river that is constantly flowing." On *Birdman*, movement replaces the cut: "most of the editing is in the way the camera moves and the way the actor moves."

Stillness has its own power. Ozu's static compositions embody what Schrader called *mu*, "the concept of negation, emptiness." A suddenly locked-down camera after movement creates what one analysis described as "transfixing" attention.

## The eloquence of empty space

Negative space -- deliberate placement of nothing around something -- is cinematography's most counterintuitive tool. As cinematographer Les Gaddis articulated: "Negative space is not 'nothing.' It is not absence, but a quiet force. It is the breath between thoughts."

Deakins is the modern master. In *Prisoners*, vast emptiness surrounds Jackman as he unravels -- negative space is despair made visible. In *Sicario*, he held a static shot of soldiers descending into darkness long after they disappeared: "Because he holds it, and the guys disappear, and then he still holds it with nobody in the frame -- it builds a tension that normally would be done with a lot of cuts."

Miyagawa understood negative space through **sumi-e** (Japanese ink painting), bringing "the infinite tonalities of grey" and compositions "rarely symmetrical yet perfectly balanced."

## When the frame becomes a metaphor

Kaminski: "Cinematography is the art of light and shadows, visual metaphors and nuance." He insists metaphors cannot be engineered: "You can't really preconceive certain visual metaphors, because then the symbol really isn't a symbol anymore -- it's more of a gimmick."

Storaro took the opposite approach. In *The Conformist*, sharp contrast of light and shadow "form a cage" around the protagonist -- "which visualized the conflict inside the character."

Miyagawa's decision to **shoot directly into the sun** in *Rashomon* was both innovation and metaphor. The sun, supposedly the source of clarity and truth, becomes blinding -- just like the contradictory testimonies.

Conrad Hall described his visual thinking in musical terms: "The visual language is an undulating language, and, like music it has to have its peaks and valleys." His most famous accidental metaphor on *In Cold Blood*: rain-shadow lines running down a face that look like tears -- "a happy accident" he recognized and preserved.

## What the masters agree on

Despite wildly different styles, these cinematographers share a core conviction: **the image must be invisible in service and visible in meaning.** Deakins: "You shouldn't love that shot. You should love what's happening, you should be in the story." Lubezki: "Sometimes I wish nobody would talk about how the movies are made." Morrison: "Usually, if you notice good cinematography, then the cinematographer's failing."

They share deep skepticism of technology for its own sake. Deakins: "There is an obsession with technology that I don't care for." Kaminski: "Young cinematographers don't know how to light." Almendros: "Nothing is worse than the abuse of technical devices."

And every one insists on **collaboration**. Richardson: "My career is based primarily upon finding a balance with a director and their vision." Khondji: "If I do have a style, it is to want to enter the head and heart of the director." Prieto: "The most important thing is to listen, not just with ears but with your heart."

Where they **diverge**: Deakins insists the camera should never call attention to itself; Lubezki embraces flares, blood on the lens. Willis stripped light away; Kaminski floods it. Storaro builds symbolic systems; Hall waited for accidents. These are not contradictions -- they prove visual storytelling has no single grammar, only infinite dialects.

The collected wisdom points toward one radical idea: **cinematography is not a visual art. It is an emotional one.** Prieto: "Every decision about a movie, about cinematography, about light, about camera placement, is emotionally important. And after all, what matters in life, I think, is what you feel."
