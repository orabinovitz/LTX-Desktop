# The Editor's Playbook: Deep Craft Reference

Use this when the core skill needs deeper reasoning about cut types, rhythm, performance sculpting, sound design, rescue techniques, or AI footage workflows.

---

## Cut types and when each earns its place

### J-cut and L-cut
The workhorses of professional editing. J-cut: audio from the next scene arrives before its picture. L-cut: current scene's audio continues over new visuals. They mirror how humans process the world -- we often hear something before turning to look. Without split edits, dialogue scenes feel like a tennis match. Use J-cuts to build anticipation before a scene reveal; use L-cuts to extend emotional resonance or show a listener's reaction.

### Match cut
Bridges two shots through visual similarity -- shape, movement, color, or composition -- to create symbolic meaning. Anne V. Coates created cinema's most celebrated example in *Lawrence of Arabia*: Lawrence blows out a match, cut directly to the desert sunrise. She revealed it was accidental -- "It was in the script as a dissolve but we saw it cut together. We looked and said, 'My God, it worked fantastic!'" David Lean told her: "That's nearly perfect. Take it away and make it perfect." She removed two frames.

### Jump cut
An amateur's mistake until Godard made it revolution in *Breathless*. "We took all the shots and systematically cut out whatever could be cut, while trying to maintain some rhythm." Forces the audience to focus on implications rather than continuous action. In AI-generated footage, jump cuts are often the honest choice when continuity between generations is impossible.

### Cross-cutting
Intercut between parallel actions to create suspense through juxtaposition. Nolan is its modern master -- *Inception* intercuts four dream layers, *Dunkirk* weaves three timelines. Gradually shortening each intercut segment accelerates tension toward climax, as in *The Godfather*'s baptism sequence where Michael renounces Satan while his henchmen execute his enemies.

### Smash cut
Maximum contrast between scenes for shock, comedy, or tonal shift. The abruptness is the point -- it slaps the audience from one emotional register into another.

### Invisible cut
Hidden in whip pans, darkness, matched action, or camera obstruction. Sustains the illusion of continuous takes (*1917*, *Birdman*). In AI footage, darkness and motion blur between clips can mask generation boundaries.

### Cutaway and insert
The editor's rescue tool. Cutaways hide edit points, provide context, and bridge continuity errors. Insert shots focus attention on critical details. In AI workflows, generating a few generic cutaways (hands, objects, environment details) gives you essential bridging material.

### Montage (Eisenstein)
Juxtaposed images create meaning neither shot contains alone. Proven by the Kuleshov Effect: the same neutral face reads as hungry, sad, or lustful depending on the adjacent image. This principle is especially powerful with AI-generated footage, where you control what images are placed in sequence.

## Rhythm: the blink theory and internal vs external time

### The blink theory
While editing *The Conversation*, Murch noticed his edit points consistently fell just before Gene Hackman was about to blink. His insight: blinks are psychological, not just physiological -- "People blink essentially when they get the idea of a thought. It's a way of saving to disk." The cut becomes the audience's blink: "In a sense, by cutting, you are blinking for the audience." A Japanese study confirmed audiences synchronize their blinking while watching a film -- about 70 people blink at the same moment -- but only when the film is "working."

Critically: "Never, never, never look for the blink and back up two frames and cut there. Feel the moment of the cut."

### Internal vs external rhythm
**Internal rhythm**: movement, dialogue pace, and energy within a shot. **External rhythm**: the cuts themselves. These must be in productive tension. As Tichenor articulates: "You set up a rhythm and then you need to break that rhythm, because that's what gets people's attention."

Tom Cross on *La La Land*: musical scenes were "like breaths -- the movie exhales and becomes wider," while realistic sections used "staccato cutting" for claustrophobia. Finding the calibration was "like trying to stick a landing on a very narrow runway."

### Murch's standing technique
Murch edits standing up -- not for ergonomics but rhythm: "I want to feel the rhythm of the scene -- just like a sax player who jumps to his feet for a solo." He cuts in real time, never frame-by-frame, letting his body respond to the footage's natural pulse. After making a cut, he watches again and tries to make the same cut. If the two land on different frames, he hasn't found the scene's true rhythm.

### Silence as rhythm
Nat Sanders on *Moonlight*: "All that silence and those spaces were working and we didn't need to try to pace it up." Murch: lingering on a character after they finish speaking tells the audience to judge their words -- "the character was probably lying." Cutting away before they finish invites the audience to take dialogue at face value.

### Pacing as variety, not speed
Lumet: "If a picture is edited in the same tempo for its entire length, it will feel much longer. It doesn't matter if five cuts per minute or five cuts every ten minutes are being used." One-note pacing, fast or slow, creates the feeling of slowness.

## Performance sculpting: the composite that never existed

### The pancake timeline (Fincher/Baxter)
Every line reading from every take is placed side by side on stacked tracks. The director selects the best reading for each individual moment. The editor assembles a composite performance that never existed in any single take.

### Split-screen compositing
Baxter: "David will tend to lock the camera off so that we have the ability to rip apart inside the frame using one performance from the back and a different performance from the front." On *Mindhunter*: "Each of the actor's takes may be from different clips. The editors will split the screen in half, take one actor from the second take, and one from the third take." Baxter estimates "99% of the time I've split the screen and sped up someone for their reaction time or to replace the take."

### Micro-timing: speed ramps and word stuffing
Subtle speed ramps condense timing between lines. "Word stuffing" transplants audio from one take onto another's visual using elastic wave retiming.

### Gesture removal (Tichenor/Ang Lee)
On *Brokeback Mountain*, Heath Ledger was "so naturally warm, he would touch people on the elbow" -- contradicting the character's internal torment. By removing merely 12 frames from one pivotal sequence, "it was a startling difference -- the character transformed into a cold, fucked-up person who was really torturing himself inside."

### Hidden gems (McMillon)
In *Moonlight*'s diner scene, McMillon found a take where "Trevante Rhodes takes several beats before slightly glancing at Jharrel Jerome." The editor's role: "preservation -- trying to preserve these moments and these beats and these looks, also looking for the hidden gems that sometimes actors will give you."

### Blink manipulation
By manipulating when an actor's blinks are visible or concealed, editors alter perceived intention, determination, deception, or vulnerability.

### The principle
Schoonmaker: "The priority is absolutely on the best take for performance. If you look at films throughout history, you will see enormous continuity errors everywhere. Even in *The Red Shoes* there are enormous continuity bumps, and it doesn't matter. Because you're being carried along by the power of the film."

Baxter: "It doesn't matter what the intention was; it only matters what was captured."

## Sound design as editorial architecture

### Murch's worldizing
Re-record studio music through speakers in real-world acoustic spaces, then combine original and environmental recording. Gives music a sense of place that studio recording alone cannot achieve.

### Horizontal and vertical thinking
Horizontal: sequential sound flow. Vertical: simultaneous layers. "There might be background freeway, birds singing, a plane overhead, footsteps. The beauty of the sound editor's work, like a musician's, is the creation and integration of a multidimensional tapestry."

### The temp music trap
"Temp love" -- filmmakers become so attached to placeholder music they cannot accept anything different. Kubrick used classical temp for *2001* and rejected Alex North's completed score at the premiere. On *Arrival*, temp attachment partially replaced Johannsson's score.

Practical advice: avoid distinctive scores as temp (one editor maintains a "No Zimmer" rule). Use temp to communicate emotional intention, not as a finished soundtrack.

### Silence as positive space
Coates removed all suspenseful music from Omar Sharif's mirage introduction in *Lawrence of Arabia*. The approaching figure is more mysterious because "we don't know his intentions" and silence forces the audience to fill the void. Murch's ideal: "The perfect sound film has zero tracks. You try to get the audience to a point where they hear the sound in their minds."

### The dialogue edit pipeline
Split mic sources onto individual tracks. Select the best mic per line. Smooth background noise cut to cut. Fill gaps with room tone -- never digital silence, which sounds fake. Flag unsalvageable lines for ADR. Concentrate on making good edits before applying processing.

## AI video editing: the full workflow

### The constraint
AI clips are single takes: no coverage, no angles, no reshoots. Every clip is essentially a limited-coverage shoot. This makes storyboard-first planning essential.

### Character consistency pipeline
1. Create a high-quality master image per character
2. Use that reference to anchor every subsequent generation
3. Generate hero close-ups first -- consistency cascades outward
4. Favor simple silhouettes and solid colors (a red jacket anchors identity better than complex patterns)
5. Test 1-2 second snippets at each shot type before committing to full generation

### Continuity across clips
1. Use last-frame extraction: the final frame of one clip becomes the first-frame input for the next
2. Lock lighting direction and color temperature in prompts for adjacent shots
3. When continuity is impossible, lean into jump cuts as intentional style
4. Generate generic cutaways (hands, objects, environments) for bridging material

### The 5-10-1 rule
Generate 5 variations on cheaper/faster models. Select the best. Iterate 10 refinements on that direction. Render once on the premium model. Explore before committing.

### Prompt craft for editable footage
Six layers: subject and action, shot type and framing, camera movement, lighting and atmosphere, technical specs (lens, depth of field), duration. Using cinematography terminology dramatically improves output. Focus each prompt on one core action. For image-to-video, describe motion only since the image already conveys visual information.

### Sound considerations for AI video
Most AI-generated clips are silent. Assemble the rough edit first focusing on
visual rhythm and pacing. Use `add_subtitle` for any text/dialogue overlays.
Audio assets can be imported via `import_media` if the user has them.

### Color unification
AI generations shift palette even with identical prompts. Apply a consistent LUT across all clips as baseline. Fine-tune individual shots. Always check skin tones manually -- AI frequently produces unnatural skin colors.

### Working with all-AI footage
All shots are AI-generated single takes. Wide shots and establishing shots
tend to generate cleanly; close-ups of faces require more iteration.
Generate hero close-ups first as character references, then use consistent
descriptions across wider shots. When a generation has artifacts, regenerate
rather than attempting to fix -- the 5-10-1 rule (5 variations, 10
refinements, 1 final render) is more efficient than fixing broken output.

## The good-to-great gap

### Restraint
Goldenberg on *22 July*: "We didn't want to politicize a point of view. Understanding that accumulated effect would be strong, it was important to be careful about not overplaying moments." Walker cutting beloved material from *Dune*: "None of those decisions are made lightly. It's torture." Murch: the German word for poetry is *Dichtung*, meaning compression.

### Openness
Riegel: "For every ten notes I get, I may disagree with nine. One is going to be something I never thought of. You have to push through the other nine to get to that one." Baxter: "I don't seek to be finished, and I remain curious with the material. I don't work from a defensive standpoint."

### Roughness as style
Schoonmaker: "We like a certain roughness sometimes. Hollywood editors tend to like a slick style where all the bumps are removed. But sometimes Marty and I like to keep in those bumps, because they add a certain grittiness, a certain reality."

### Sensory editing
Walker's concept: working at the "brain stem" level to create images that resonate because "they're very sensory and very immersive." The great editor creates what Murch calls "a sustained deja vu": "You don't know what's going to happen next, but when it does happen, you feel, 'Yes, that's exactly what happened.'"
