# Directing Principles: What the Masters Share

Distilled from the working philosophies of Kubrick, Scorsese, Fincher, Malick, Bergman, Kurosawa, Bong Joon-ho, Cuaron, Wong Kar-wai, Lumet, Nolan, PTA, Coppola, Ramsay, and Bresson. Use this when you need deeper reasoning about actor work, scene construction, subtext, preparation, tonal control, or the director's relationship with editing and sound.

---

## A. The actor spectrum: control, collaboration, and radical freedom

Master directors span a dramatic continuum in how they work with actors, yet each arrives at the same destination: emotional truth on screen.

### The many-takes method (Kubrick, Fincher)
Kubrick demanded 30-127 takes per scene. The goal: suppress conscious technique and reach a deeper place. Kidman explained that dozens of takes "had the effect of suppressing an actor's conscious thoughts about technique, diffusing the concentration Kubrick said he could see in the eyes." Fincher frames it differently: "I hate earnestness in performance. Usually by Take 17 the earnestness is gone."

### Torpedoing and improvisation (Malick, Wong Kar-wai)
Malick dismantles conventional actor-director dynamics. Actors receive no script pages, no lines. He calls it "torpedoing" -- throwing characters into scenes without other actors' advance knowledge. Fassbender: "You're just trying to survive." Wong Kar-wai works without a finished script, writing dialogue the morning of shooting. Leung: "A lot of things between me and Tony are beyond words."

### Trust-based improvisation within structure (Scorsese, Lumet)
The "You talkin' to me?" mirror scene emerged from a script direction that read simply: "Travis looks in the mirror." Scorsese crouched below the lens, encouraging De Niro. "It was like a jazz riff. Just like a solo." Lumet championed two full weeks of rehearsal before cameras rolled and used improvisation as a rehearsal tool -- always in service of the script, never replacing it.

### Emotional simplicity over intellectual analysis (Bong, Coppola)
Bong directs through feeling: "I don't talk about political commentary. I always say things like, 'Don't you feel sorry for this character?'" Sofia Coppola: "Instead of saying, 'Act tired in this scene,' I'll say, 'You've been up all night and you want to go home,' to set the mood."

### Key principles
- Casting is 85-90% of the picture (Scorsese)
- A director can't get anything out of an actor that he doesn't already have (Kubrick)
- Only someone who is well prepared has the opportunity to improvise (Bergman)
- All good work requires self-revelation (Lumet)

## B. Scene construction: spatial logic and emotional architecture

### Lumet's lens plot
For *12 Angry Men*, Lumet designed progressive compression: starting with 28-40mm lenses, progressing to 50mm, 75mm, 100mm as the room should seem smaller. First third shot above eye level, second third at eye level, last third below eye level -- ceiling appearing, walls closing in. The final exterior used a wider lens than any in the film, from the highest angle, designed to "literally give us all air."

### Bong's architectural approach
Bong called *Parasite* a "staircase movie." Both houses were built entirely from scratch to enable vertical compositions embodying class hierarchy. Lines created by window joinery visually separate poor from privileged. Every spatial choice is a social statement. "When I find a great location, I feel as excited as if I'd found a great actor."

### Cuaron's durational construction
The long take forces audiences to experience time with characters rather than being edited through it. "We are trying to avoid editing seeking for an effect. Rather than that, we tried to create moments of truthfulness and to have the camera registering that moment of truthfulness."

### Wong Kar-wai's repetition as structure
In *In the Mood for Love*, recurring slow-motion passages on the stairway accumulate weight through visual rhythm rather than dialogue. Role-playing rehearsals blurred the line: "By the end, even they couldn't separate performance from truth."

## C. The power of withholding

The most powerful moments in cinema are often defined by what is absent.

**Kubrick**: "If you really want to communicate something, the least effective and least enjoyable way is directly. It only goes in about an inch. But if you can get people to the point where they have to think a moment what it is you're getting at, and then discover it, the thrill of discovery goes right through the heart."

**Bresson**: "Hide the ideas, but so that people find them. The most important will be the most hidden."

**Tarkovsky**: "When less than everything has been said about a subject, you can still think on further."

**Wong Kar-wai** constructed *In the Mood for Love* around what is hidden: "We're not making a movie about what's said -- we're making one about what's hidden." He cut the sex scene: "What interested me was people's behavior under the circumstances, their way of keeping or sharing secrets."

**Ramsay** practices subtext through absence of violence. In *You Were Never Really Here*, actual violence happens off-screen or through CCTV footage. "I wanted to approach it in a different way, to make it exciting and intense but not sexy and cool."

**Cuaron** embeds subtext in camera behavior. In *Roma*, the camera follows Cleo but consistently lags behind -- "this cinematic choice seems to suggest that although she is established in the family's lives, she's never quite able to catch up to them."

## D. Sound, music, and silence as directing tools

### Music as narrative architecture
**Kubrick** used pre-existing classical music because "however good our best film composers may be, they are not a Beethoven." After Kubrick, music became "absolutely an essential part of the narrative, intellectual drive of the film." *2001* deploys only forty minutes of dialogue in two hours and nineteen minutes.

**Scorsese** designs entire films from music outward: "Most of the shots I design and most of the way I approach any scene comes from music. I usually put myself in a room for about eight or nine days with music and design a picture on the page."

**Nolan** inverts the process. For *Interstellar*, he gave Zimmer a one-page fable about a father leaving a child -- no mention of space -- and said: "You get one day, just write something." That fragile piece became the heart of the film.

### Sound design as character
**Ramsay** integrates sound at the script stage: "We talk about sound being almost like a camera. We focus on this sound, this detail, and we get inside this character." A sprinkler becomes a sound of horror through repetition and association.

### The eloquence of silence
**Bergman's** "silence of God" trilogy dramatizes what happens when the ultimate interlocutor stops speaking.

**Bresson**: "The ear is profound, whereas the eye is frivolous, too easily satisfied. The ear is active, imaginative, whereas the eye is passive." His instruction: "Against the tactics of speed, of noise, set tactics of slowness, of silence."

## E. Tonal control and genre mixing

**Bong Joon-ho** pivots from comedy to horror within a single scene by grounding everything in character: "It's not as if I divide the script into sequences for the actors: This is the horror section, this is the comedy section. The central core of the story were always these characters."

His most revealing insight: "In life, if you take a crime scene, it depends on the distance you are from it. When you're close in it, it can be sad and scary and terrifying, but take a step back, there are comic moments." Genre is a function of proximity, not category.

**Lumet** drew the sharpest line between drama and melodrama: "In drama, the characters should determine the story. In melodrama, the story determines the characters." And: "Inevitability is the key. In a well-made drama, I want to feel: 'Of course -- that's where it was heading all along.' And yet the inevitability mustn't eliminate surprise."

**Fincher** refuses comfort: "Entertainment has to come hand in hand with a little bit of medicine. Some people go to the movies to be reminded that everything's okay. I don't make those kinds of movies."

## F. Preparation methods

### Coppola's Godfather Notebook
Razor-bladed every page of Puzo's novel into a notebook. For each of 50 scenes, analyzed: synopsis, historical context, imagery and tone, emotional core, pitfalls to avoid. "The script was really an unnecessary document. I could have made the movie just from this notebook." His motive: "In truth, I think I made the notebook out of profound fear."

### Bong's storyboards
Every frame drawn by hand, published as a graphic novel, 99% fidelity to the final cut. Evans: "Director Bong has storyboards laid out so that he has already edited it in his brain. It's like building a house and instead of needing a bag of nails, saying 'I need 53 nails.'" Yet Bong resists elevating the method: "I actually storyboard to quell my own anxiety."

### Kurosawa's notebooks
"I've forgotten who it was that said creation is memory. I have always kept a notebook handy when I read a book. I write down my reactions and what particularly moves me. I have stacks of these notebooks, and when I go off to write a script, these are what I read."

### Lumet's foundational question
"What is this movie about?" From that answer flows every technical decision. "The theme (the WHAT of the movie) is going to determine the style (the HOW of the movie)."

### Malick and Lubezki's dogma
Shooting rules from an unmade Che Guevara biopic: capture life through a character's POV; handheld camera; only existing light. "Once you start shooting without film lights, and you go twenty days without using an HMI, if you then put up film lights, they look really bad. So we burned our bridges."

### Bergman's spear
"I make all my decisions on intuition. I throw a spear into the darkness. That is intuition. Then I must send an army into the darkness to find the spear. That is intellect."

## G. The editing mind

### Murch's Rule of Six
Priority order for cuts, weighted: emotion (51%), story (23%), rhythm (10%), eye-trace (7%), two-dimensional plane (5%), three-dimensional continuity (4%). "If you find you have to sacrifice certain of those six things to make a cut, sacrifice your way up, item by item, from the bottom."

### Pacing as variety, not speed
**Lumet**: "If a picture is edited in the same tempo for its entire length, it will feel much longer. It doesn't matter if five cuts per minute or five cuts every ten minutes are being used."

### Roughness as style
**Scorsese/Schoonmaker**: "We like a certain roughness sometimes. Hollywood editors tend to like a slick style where all the bumps are removed. But sometimes we like to keep in those bumps, because they add a certain grittiness, a certain reality."

### Pre-editing through storyboards
**Bong**: *Parasite* contains only 960 cuts -- remarkably few for a two-hour thriller. Because Bong knows precisely where he will cut, he shoots no traditional overlapping coverage.

### Objectivity
**Kurosawa**: "No matter how much difficulty you had in obtaining a particular shot, the audience will never know. If it is not interesting, it simply isn't interesting. You must be objective enough to cut it."

### Film discovered in editing
**Malick**: "We would explore all the different possibilities for editing a scene. How could we change the perspective, and in turn change which character we identify with?"
**Wong Kar-wai**: Restructures entire narratives in post. Step-printing emerged as "an answer to John Woo's use of slow motion."

## H. What unites them

**Theme drives technique** -- from Lumet's systematic questioning to Kubrick's insistence that "the feel of the experience is the important thing."

**Emotion supersedes technical correctness** -- Murch places it at 51%, more than all other factors combined.

**Suggestion exceeds statement** -- Murch counsels "the most with the least," Bresson demands "the most important will be the most hidden," Tarkovsky instructs "as little as possible has actually to be shown."

**The deepest divide** runs between control (Kubrick, Fincher, Bong -- the film exists fully in the mind before cameras roll) and discovery (Malick, Wong Kar-wai -- the film can only be found through making it). Between these poles, Scorsese, Cuaron, PTA, and Bergman occupy productive middle ground: rigorous preparation that creates space for accident. Lumet: "All great work is preparing yourself for the accident to happen."

Scorsese: "The most important thing I can convey to you is that you always have to find your own way." Bong, borrowing from Scorsese: "The most personal is the most creative."
