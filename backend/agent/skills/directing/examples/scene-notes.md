# Scene Notes Examples

## Example 1 — scene diagnosis

User:
"Help me direct a breakup scene in a kitchen. Two people are calm on the surface, but one already knows it's over."

A:
### Dramatic center
One person is trying to preserve dignity. The other is trying to delay guilt. The scene is not about the breakup itself; it's about who will force the truth into the room.

### POV and emotional alignment
Keep us mostly with the person who already knows. The audience should feel the exhaustion of waiting for honesty, not the surprise of the breakup.

### Actor direction
For the one ending it:
- play restraint, not cruelty
- speak as if every line is chosen to avoid mess
- let the body reveal the real decision before the words do

For the other:
- do not play ignorance
- play the dawning recognition that they are being managed
- let small practical actions become self-protection

### Subtext and silence
The key beat is the pause before the first honest sentence. Do not rush it. Let the audience watch both people decide whether to continue the lie.

### Visual strategy
Keep hands busy with ordinary kitchen tasks. A mug, towel, or drawer can carry tension better than added dialogue. Let the room feel too normal for the emotional event.

### Pacing adjustments
Enter late. Cut the warm-up lines. Hold longer after the sentence that makes denial impossible.

### Specific suggestion
Remove any line that explains the history of the relationship. The scene becomes stronger when the audience reads the breakup through behavior and timing.

## Example 2 — rewriting a confrontation beat

User:
"This argument scene feels flat. Make it more cinematic."

A:
It feels flat because the scene is currently verbal but not behavioral. Both characters are stating their position instead of trying to win, hide, wound, or escape.

### Fix the scene's engine
Give each character a tactic:
- Character A: regain control without admitting fear
- Character B: force a confession without appearing needy

### Actor direction
Do not play "anger." Play:
- interruption as dominance
- stillness as accusation
- politeness as aggression

### Silence
After the most damaging line, do not answer immediately. Let the silence become the accusation.

### Visual metaphor
Put one character near the exit and the other deeper in the room. Their spatial relationship should express what the dialogue is circling: one wants out, one wants reckoning.

### Pacing
Trim the first 20 percent of the scene and delay the harshest line. The scene will feel stronger if escalation happens through withheld pressure first.

### One-line principle
Make the audience watch the power change hands.
