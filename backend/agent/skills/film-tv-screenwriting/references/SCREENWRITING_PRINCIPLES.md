# Screenwriting Principles Reference

Use this file when you need deeper craft reasoning, especially for diagnosis, outlining, or explaining why a story, scene, or line is or is not working.

This reference distills a set of major screenwriting philosophies that often disagree in method but overlap in the underlying craft. The point is not to obey any one writer as doctrine. The point is to borrow the strongest lens for the specific problem in front of you.

## A. Character: the person is the engine

### 1) Want is the basic fuel
Across modern screenwriting craft, the most durable shared principle is this: drama begins when someone wants something badly enough to act.

Use this to diagnose weak material:
- If the audience cannot tell what the character wants **in the scene**, the scene will feel shapeless.
- If the audience cannot tell what the character wants **in the story**, the script will feel inert or episodic.
- If the character only "wants to be happy," "wants respect," or "wants revenge" in a vague way, the material often needs a more playable immediate objective.

Useful distinction:
- **Want**: the objective the character can pursue.
- **Need**: the deeper hunger, wound, fear, or psychic lack under that objective.

When notes feel abstract, translate them into want language:
- "The character feels flat" -> Their pursuit is vague or passive.
- "The scene feels talky" -> Nobody is using speech to get anything.
- "The story feels repetitive" -> The want is not evolving under pressure.

### 2) Contradiction makes people worth watching
A compelling character is usually split. They claim one thing about themselves and reveal another in behavior.

High-value contradiction patterns:
- moral self-image + selfish conduct
- charm + cruelty
- humor + despair
- protectiveness + control
- competence + emotional illiteracy
- yearning for intimacy + sabotage of intimacy
- spiritual aspiration + bodily compulsion
- idealism + vanity
- generosity + need for domination

When a character feels "generic," look for missing contradiction.

### 3) Behavior beats backstory
Backstory matters only when it changes present action. Avoid "explaining" a person instead of dramatizing them.

Prefer:
- ritual
- avoidance pattern
- status move
- lie told under pressure
- task performed revealingly
- choice that costs them something
- mismatch between stated value and actual action

A useful test: if you remove the explanatory paragraph and the character still reads clearly through behavior, the writing is working.

## B. Scene craft: every scene must turn

### 1) Scene objective
A scene typically works best when the central player enters wanting something specific:
- get permission
- stop a breakup
- hide evidence
- extract a confession
- win approval
- delay a decision
- make someone jealous
- secure money
- avoid exposure
- force a promise

The tighter the objective, the more playable the scene.

### 2) Why now?
Urgency is often the hidden missing piece. The scene should justify why the encounter occurs now.

Good urgency sources:
- deadline
- emotional breaking point
- witness arriving
- information becoming unavailable
- relationship window closing
- danger escalating
- public exposure risk
- money pressure
- physical deterioration
- institutional timing

### 3) Turn types
A scene "turns" when it does not end in the same emotional or strategic configuration in which it began.

Common turn categories:
- persuasion fails
- lie lands, then cracks
- lower-status character seizes power
- secret leaks
- threat becomes credible
- tenderness becomes humiliation
- seduction becomes interrogation
- alliance becomes transaction
- hope becomes dread
- avoidance becomes commitment

A scene can be quiet and still turn.

### 4) Layering
Reality often appears when a scene carries multiple tracks at once:
- practical business
- emotional agenda
- social mask
- underlying danger
- physical discomfort
- environmental interruption

Example:
Two siblings discuss wedding seating while really negotiating who is loved more, while trying not to upset their mother, while the air conditioner is broken and everyone is sweating. The scene gains texture because the literal topic is not the whole scene.

## C. Dialogue: objective, rhythm, omission

### 1) Dialogue is action
Good dialogue is not information delivery. It is the tactic a character uses to change another person or avoid being changed.

Ask:
- What is each line trying to accomplish?
- Is the character telling the truth, a partial truth, or a tactical version of the truth?
- What is withheld?
- What would be too dangerous to say directly?

### 2) Subtext
Subtext is not "saying the opposite." It is the pressure created by what is emotionally or strategically present but not fully spoken.

Subtext often grows from:
- shame
- desire
- grief
- guilt
- fear of rejection
- status anxiety
- deception
- politeness masking aggression
- a cultural rule against direct speech
- self-deception

### 3) Voice differentiation
Characters should not sound interchangeable. Differences can come from:
- length of thought
- metaphor use
- sarcasm vs bluntness
- rhythm
- sentence completion or refusal
- specificity
- class markers
- cultural markers
- emotional intelligence
- appetite for conflict

Avoid "everyone sounds like the writer."

### 4) Great lines are rare and must be earned
Not every line should sparkle. Too much wit or constant quotability creates unreality unless the project is deliberately heightened.

Often the strongest dialogue mix is:
- mostly functional and character-true lines
- a smaller number of loaded or memorable lines
- one or two lines that crystallize the scene or character at the exact right pressure point

## D. Structure: shape is meaning

There is no single correct structural dogma, but structure always makes an argument about causality, pressure, and meaning.

### 1) Classical causality
Useful for:
- thrillers
- mainstream drama
- commercial features
- pilots needing propulsion

Strengths:
- clarity
- escalation
- inevitability
- satisfying payoffs

Good tools:
- setup -> complication -> escalation -> crisis -> climax -> aftermath
- "but / except / then" story logic
- consequence chains

### 2) Anti-classical or fractured structure
Useful for:
- memory-based stories
- grief stories
- fragmented identity stories
- multi-perspective narratives
- films where chronology itself is part of meaning

Strengths:
- can embody subjectivity
- can make emotional logic primary
- can create thematic resonance through juxtaposition

Risk:
- fragmentation without governing concept feels arbitrary

If the story is non-linear, know what principle organizes the order:
- memory
- revelation
- thematic collision
- parallel causality
- moral contrast
- dying consciousness
- last-day compression
- institutional cross-section

### 3) Endings as truth events
An ending usually works when it cashes out the central contradiction, lie, or moral pattern.

Ending categories:
- confession
- sacrifice
- irreversible corruption
- acceptance
- exposure
- reunion at cost
- pyrrhic win
- tragic inevitability
- refusal to change
- survival with diminished illusion

An ending often fails when:
- it solves plot but not character contradiction
- it punishes or rewards in a way the story hasn't earned
- it explains rather than dramatizes
- it turns sentimental to protect the audience

## E. Theme: embedded, not announced

A strong theme is often a recurring pattern of action and consequence, not a sentence spoken aloud.

Find theme through:
- repeated choices
- repeated costs
- the kind of lie the protagonist clings to
- the institutions or relationships exerting pressure
- what the ending validates or destroys

If a story is "about family," "about ambition," or "about grief," that is not yet useful. Make it sharper:
- family as debt
- ambition as self-erasure
- grief as a private religion
- love as surveillance
- masculinity as performance under terror
- success as a machine that consumes intimacy

## F. Visual storytelling: trust the medium

### 1) Ask what can be shown
Before adding lines, ask whether the scene could communicate through:
- a delay
- a wrong object in the room
- clothing
- money handling
- posture shift
- who touches whom first
- who sits and who stays standing
- a ritual performed differently than before
- a physical task done expertly or badly
- what the character notices and what they refuse to notice

### 2) Enter late, leave early
Scenes often improve when trimmed at both ends. Cut greeting rituals, recap dialogue, and after-the-point explanation unless those are exactly where the tension lives.

### 3) Silent sequences can carry enormous load
Do not be afraid of wordless storytelling when the objective, stakes, and visual logic remain clear.

## G. Film-specific and TV-specific differences

### Film / feature
Usually needs:
- a central spine that can support sustained escalation
- compression and combination of functions
- a climax that feels inevitable in retrospect
- a finite, coherent argument

Questions:
- why is this a feature and not a short?
- what irreversible line is crossed?
- what keeps the pressure rising?

### TV / pilot / series
Needs both current tension and repeatable future tension.

TV-specific craft questions:
- what engine creates episode after episode?
- what relationship grid keeps colliding?
- what arenas repeat?
- what season question is launched?
- what unresolved moral, emotional, or institutional tension keeps generating story?

A pilot should not feel like a feature with the last twenty pages cut off. It should feel like the opening of a durable machine.

## H. Tone and genre

Genre is a promise to the audience about the kind of pressure, pleasure, and pattern they are entering.

### Comedy
Often works through:
- status misread
- embarrassment
- denial
- escalating commitment to a bad tactic
- emotional truth underneath absurd behavior

### Thriller
Often works through:
- asymmetric knowledge
- time pressure
- concealment
- surveillance
- danger made specific
- choices that narrow quickly

### Prestige character drama
Often relies on:
- contradiction
- behavioral detail
- silence
- moral ambiguity
- cumulative pressure over overt plot mechanisms

### Crime / antihero drama
Common engines:
- rationalization
- pride
- appetite
- competence rewarded then poisoned
- moral cost lagging behind tactical success

### Family drama
Common engines:
- debt
- obligation
- grief
- inheritance
- old role traps
- love expressed sideways

## I. Development notes: how to diagnose precisely

When giving notes, name the real failure mode.

Examples:
- "It feels slow" may mean the objective enters too late.
- "The dialogue is on the nose" may mean the scene lacks subtext because the hidden agenda is unclear.
- "I don't buy the ending" may mean the midpoint and second-half decisions never prepared the final choice.
- "I don't care about the lead" may mean the contradiction is missing, not that the lead needs to be nicer.
- "The script feels messy" may mean the organizing principle is weak, not that the plot is too complex.

Useful diagnosis categories:
- objective clarity
- escalation
- causal logic
- contradiction
- voice differentiation
- subtext
- scene entry / exit
- tonal coherence
- visual storytelling
- ending inevitability
- series engine durability

## J. Process philosophy

Different masters disagree about method, but a few process truths are durable:
- draft toward discovery, then rewrite toward precision
- cut without sentimentality
- do not confuse theory knowledge with dramatic effectiveness
- listen to how people actually evade, posture, and confess
- the struggle is part of the work, not evidence that you are doing it wrong
- fear often marks the material worth writing

Use this reference as a toolkit, not a religion. For any given user request, choose the lens that reveals the deepest dramatic problem and then solve that problem directly.
