# Output Examples

Use these as pattern examples, not rigid templates. Adapt structure to the user's request.

## Example 1: Scene diagnosis

**User request:** "Why does this breakup scene feel flat?"

**Strong answer pattern:**

The real problem is not the breakup itself. It is that neither person is still trying to get something from the other, so the scene has no leverage.

What is missing:
- She has already emotionally accepted the breakup before the scene starts.
- He is mostly explaining himself instead of pursuing a live objective.
- The subtext is thin because both characters are naming the topic directly.
- The scene ends in the same emotional shape it begins in: resignation.

What would make it dramatic:
- Give him a desperate objective beyond "talk": get her to delay moving out until after the funeral.
- Give her a conflicting hidden agenda: she wants to leave, but she also wants him to finally admit what he did.
- Let the literal conversation be about boxes, keys, the lease, or the cat — not "our relationship" for three pages.
- Build to a turn: he lies to stay in control, then accidentally reveals the exact thing that confirms she has to go.

A more dramatic scene question:
Can he keep her from leaving tonight without admitting the truth that caused her to leave?

That question gives the scene pressure, tactics, and a real turn.

## Example 2: Rewrite framing

**User request:** "Rewrite this dialogue so it feels more HBO and less expositional."

**Strong answer pattern:**

I'm changing three things:
1. cutting direct explanation
2. giving each speaker a different agenda
3. letting status and resentment shape the rhythm

### Rewrite

MARA
You came early.

DAVID
You said six.

MARA
I said "after six." You always hear the half that saves you.

DAVID
I'm not doing this at the door.

MARA
No, you usually wait until there's an audience.

DAVID looks past her into the apartment.
The table is set for three.

DAVID
He's here?

MARA
You tell me. You're the one who taught him how to arrive before the apology.

DAVID
Mara—

MARA
Don't use the soft voice. That's for strangers and juries.

### Why it works better
- The scene topic is arrival and dinner, not "our history."
- The resentment lives underneath the lines instead of being explained.
- Each line is trying to gain position, not share information.
- The detail of the third place setting introduces pressure visually.

## Example 3: Pilot engine breakout

**User request:** "Outline a one-hour pilot about an elite family restaurant empire starting to collapse."

**Strong answer pattern:**

### Core series engine
A gifted but self-destructive heir returns to save the family's restaurant group just as a hidden financial disaster, an aging patriarch, and a labor revolt threaten to expose that the dynasty was always a performance.

### Why the show has legs
This is not just "can they save the business?" The durable engine is:
- every location is a power arena
- every service night creates new pressure
- family love and corporate control are fused
- secrets from the company's rise keep poisoning present decisions
- staff, siblings, investors, and press all want incompatible outcomes

### Pilot spine
- The heir is dragged back for a celebration service honoring the founder.
- During the event, a freezer failure, an employee injury, and an investor surprise visit expose deep instability.
- The lead discovers the books were manipulated and the brand may be insolvent.
- A sibling believes the lead is using the crisis to seize control.
- The patriarch collapses after service but may have hidden the financial rot on purpose.

### Pilot ending
The lead agrees to stay and take over operations — then learns the labor organizer is their former lover and already has enough evidence to destroy the family publicly.

That ending launches the season instead of merely ending an episode.

## Example 4: Character construction

**User request:** "Help me build a memorable antihero for a crime drama."

**Strong answer pattern:**

Do not start with "cool criminal traits." Start with contradiction.

### Core contradiction
He believes he is a protector. In practice, he ruins the people he protects by deciding everything for them.

### Public self
Soft-spoken neighborhood problem-solver. Generous. Reliable. Good with elders and kids.

### Private hunger
He needs to be indispensable. If people stop needing him, he feels erased.

### Behavioral tells
- fixes other people's problems before they ask
- gives gifts that create debt
- never asks directly for love or loyalty
- becomes cold, not loud, when betrayed
- keeps immaculate records because "someone has to"

### Story danger
His competence makes him seductive to the audience and to other characters, but every solution he offers increases dependency and control.

That gives you a character who can carry scenes, not just a collection of genre signals.
