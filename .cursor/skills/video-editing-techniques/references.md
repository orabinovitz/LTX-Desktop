# Video Editing Techniques — Extended Reference

## Murch's Rule of Six (Detailed)

Walter Murch's criteria for an ideal cut, from *In the Blink of an Eye*:

1. **Emotion (51%)** — The cut is true to the emotion of the moment. This is the hardest to define but the most important. If the cut *feels* wrong, it is wrong, regardless of whether it satisfies every other criterion.

2. **Story (23%)** — The cut advances the story. Every shot should either push the narrative forward or provide essential context. If a shot does neither, it should be cut entirely.

3. **Rhythm (10%)** — The cut happens at a rhythmically interesting and "right" moment. Editing has a musicality — cuts fall on beats, and the internal rhythm of shot durations creates a tempo the viewer subconsciously follows.

4. **Eye trace (7%)** — The cut respects the audience's focus of interest. Where are they looking when the cut happens? The next shot should acknowledge that gaze position.

5. **Two-dimensional plane of screen (5%)** — The cut respects the grammar of the screen rectangle. Screen direction (left-to-right, up-to-down), composition balance, and the established visual vocabulary of the sequence.

6. **Three-dimensional space (4%)** — The cut is true to the physical/spatial relationships established within the scene. The 180° rule, spatial logic, and consistent geography.

**Key insight:** You should be willing to sacrifice items at the bottom of the list to preserve items at the top. A technically "incorrect" cut that serves emotion and story is better than a technically perfect cut that feels dead.

## The Kuleshov Effect

Lev Kuleshov's 1920s experiment demonstrating that meaning in cinema is constructed through editing, not individual shots.

The same expressionless face intercut with:
- A bowl of soup → viewer reads hunger
- A child in a coffin → viewer reads grief
- A woman on a couch → viewer reads desire

**Implication:** The shot *before* and *after* any given image determines its emotional meaning. This is the foundation of intellectual montage and is why shot order in a timeline matters enormously.

## The 30-Degree Rule

When cutting between two shots of the same subject, the camera angle should change by at least 30 degrees. Smaller changes create a jarring "jump" that reads as an error rather than an intentional edit.

**When to break it:** Jump cuts intentionally violate this rule for stylistic effect (showing time compression, disorientation, or raw documentary energy).

## The 180-Degree Rule (Axis of Action)

An imaginary line between two subjects (the "axis") establishes screen direction. Keeping the camera on one side of this line maintains spatial consistency:
- Character A always faces screen-right
- Character B always faces screen-left

**Crossing the line** reverses screen direction, disorienting the viewer. Acceptable when:
- A neutral shot (directly on the axis) bridges the cross
- The camera visibly moves across the line in a single shot
- Intentional disorientation is the goal

## Shot Size Progression

Standard shot sizes from widest to tightest:

| Abbreviation | Name | Shows |
|-------------|------|-------|
| EWS | Extreme wide shot | Landscape, location context |
| WS | Wide shot | Full figure in environment |
| MWS | Medium wide shot | Knees up |
| MS | Medium shot | Waist up |
| MCU | Medium close-up | Chest up |
| CU | Close-up | Face fills frame |
| ECU | Extreme close-up | Detail (eyes, hands, props) |

**Editing convention:** Cuts that jump more than two sizes (e.g., WS → ECU) create dramatic emphasis. Cuts within one size step (e.g., MS → MCU) feel subtle and conversational.

## Continuity Editing Principles

The "invisible" style that dominates narrative film. The goal is for the viewer to forget they're watching edited footage.

1. **Match on action** — Cut during movement so motion bridges the edit
2. **Eyeline match** — Cut to what a character sees
3. **Shot-reverse shot** — Standard dialogue coverage
4. **Establishing shot** — Orient the viewer in new spaces
5. **Re-establishing shot** — Return to wide when blocking changes
6. **Screen direction** — Maintain consistent left/right movement
7. **Audio continuity** — Room tone, ambient sound carries across cuts

## Common Transition Types

| Transition | Effect | Use case |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Hard cut | Instant switch | Default; within scenes |
| Dissolve / cross-dissolve | Overlapping fade | Time passage, connection between scenes |
| Fade to black | Gradual darkness | End of sequence, significant time jump |
| Fade from black | Gradual reveal | Beginning of sequence, new chapter |
| Wipe | Geometric reveal | Stylistic choice, genre-specific |
| Iris | Circular reveal/close | Vintage/comedic style |
| Whip pan | Fast pan blur | Energy, surprise, hidden cut point |
| Match dissolve | Dissolve between graphically similar images | Thematic connection |

## Audio Editing Principles

- **Room tone:** Every location has ambient sound. Cut points without matching room tone create a "dead" feeling.
- **Audio crossfade:** Even hard visual cuts benefit from 2-6 frame audio crossfades to avoid pops.
- **Dialogue editing:** Trim breaths and pauses for pacing, but keep enough to feel natural.
- **Music cuts:** Cut on downbeats or phrase boundaries. Cutting mid-phrase feels wrong.
- **Sound bridges:** Using audio from the next scene (J-cut) or previous scene (L-cut) smooths transitions.
- **Silence as punctuation:** Sudden absence of sound is as powerful as any cut.

## Rhythm and Timing

### Musical Analogy
- **Tempo** — Average shot duration (fast = short shots, slow = long shots)
- **Meter** — Regular vs. irregular shot patterns
- **Accent** — Emphasis through unusually short or long holds
- **Phrase** — A group of shots forming a complete visual thought
- **Rest** — A beat of stillness or empty frame between visual phrases

### Pacing Dynamics
A sequence that maintains one tempo becomes monotonous. Effective editing varies pacing:
- Build from slow to fast for escalation
- Drop from fast to slow for impact (the sudden quiet after chaos)
- Alternate rhythms for cross-cut sequences (each thread can have its own tempo)

## Performance Editing

When working with acted footage:
- Cut on the **best performance**, not the best camera angle
- The out-point matters more than the in-point (leave at the peak of expression)
- Reaction shots are often more important than action shots
- A held close-up on a silent face can convey more than dialogue
- When in doubt, stay on the listener, not the speaker
